Train Delay Analysis and Prediction in Lausanne

Which operational, temporal, and weather features are associated with higher train delay rates in Lausanne, and how well can those features predict whether a specific arrival will be delayed?

GitHub repository

Introduction

Train delay analysis and prediction in Lausanne.

This project studies whether temporal, operational, and weather factors are associated with train delays in the Lausanne area and whether those factors can support a supervised model for delay prediction. I combine SBB/CFF Istdaten train-arrival records with hourly Open-Meteo weather observations, construct a 90,818-row analysis dataset, run hypothesis tests, and compare six classifiers.

The strongest statistical differences are linked to scheduled hour, weekday, season, weekend status, train type, line, station, rain, temperature group, and wind group. XGBoost performs best among the tested models, reaching 0.648 test accuracy, 0.642 delayed-class F1, and 0.710 ROC-AUC.

Error analysis shows that the model is most uncertain around specific busy or transition periods: the 19:00 hour has the highest hour-level error rate, rush periods create many false delay alarms, and midday hours miss a larger share of true delays.

Motivation

Train delays affect daily mobility at a city scale.

Train delays are small events individually, but they affect daily mobility at a city scale. A few minutes of delay can change transfer plans, crowd platforms, and make a timetable feel less reliable to commuters. Lausanne is a useful case study because it has a mix of local, regional, intercity, and international train services, and because the station area is exposed to seasonal weather changes.

The project is also personally relevant because I will start my doctoral studies in Lausanne next year, so understanding the reliability of the local rail network connects directly to the city I am about to live and work in.

The first part of the question is explanatory: it focuses on patterns that can be described and tested statistically. The second part is predictive: it asks whether those same patterns can be used to construct a machine learning model for predicting delays.

Data source and collection

SBB/CFF Istdaten enriched with Open-Meteo weather.

The main data source is SBB/CFF Istdaten, the public actual-transport dataset published through OpenTransportData Switzerland. I enriched the train records with Open-Meteo historical hourly weather data for Lausanne.

Train records

The raw records contain planned and actual or predicted arrival and departure times, operator fields, line information, station names, and train descriptors. I used the official archive index to collect daily CSV files from four 30-day windows: January, April, July, and October 2025.

Weather enrichment

The weather fields used in the final dataset are temperature, precipitation, snowfall, and wind speed. Each train arrival is matched to the weather observation from the same scheduled date and scheduled arrival hour.

Final dataset

After filtering, feature engineering, and weather merging, the final dataset has 90,818 arrival-level rows and 21 columns. The target variable is delayed, where 1 means the predicted arrival time is later than the scheduled arrival time.

Data analysis pipeline

Four reproducible notebooks.

The analysis is organized as four reproducible notebooks. Each notebook writes its outputs to the repository so later stages can be inspected without rerunning the entire pipeline.

Data Preparation

The first notebook, data_collection.ipynb, performs data collection, filtering, translation, feature engineering, weather collection, and the final merge.

Exploratory Data Analysis

The second notebook, eda.ipynb, profiles the dataset and visualizes delay patterns by time, service structure, and weather. Since the target is binary, most descriptive plots use the mean of delayed as the delay rate.

Hypothesis Testing

The third notebook, hypothesis_testing.ipynb, turns the strongest EDA patterns into formal tests. Categorical features are tested with chi-square independence tests.

Machine Learning

The fourth notebook, ml_modeling.ipynb, creates an 80/20 train/test split stratified by the joint season x delayed key, repeats hypothesis tests on the training data only for feature selection, compares six models, and analyzes test set errors.

Findings

Descriptive patterns and hypothesis test results.

The dataset is close to balanced: 47.4% of arrivals are delayed and 52.6% are on time or early. Delay rates vary visibly across scheduled hour, weekday, train type, line, and station.

At the 0.05 level, the project rejects the null hypothesis for scheduled arrival hour, weekday, season, weekend status, train type, line name, temperature group, rainy versus dry hour, wind speed group, and station. The tests do not reject the operator comparison or the snowy versus non-snowy hour comparison.

Machine learning

Model comparison.

All tuned models improve over the majority-class baseline, which confirms that the selected temporal, operational, and weather features contain predictive signal. XGBoost has the best overall test performance in this experiment: 0.648 accuracy, 0.642 delayed-class F1, and 0.710 ROC-AUC. The decision tree has the highest delayed-class recall, but it gives up more specificity, meaning it predicts delays more aggressively.

Error analysis

Where the model struggles.

For the selected XGBoost model, the confusion matrix shows 6,037 true negatives, 3,515 false positives, 2,885 false negatives, and 5,727 true positives on our test set with 18,164 rows. This means the model catches about two thirds of actual delayed arrivals, but it still creates many false delay alarms.

I grouped test set predictions by scheduled arrival hour and by broad arrival periods. The highest hourly error rate occurs at 19:00, where 38.9% of test rows are misclassified. This hour belongs to the PM rush window. AM rush arrivals have a 34.7% overall error rate, but the main issue is false positives: 45.1% of on-time or early AM-rush arrivals are predicted as delayed.

Midday has a different failure mode. Its overall error rate is 35.9%, but the false-negative rate among actual delays is 50.2%. In other words, during midday the model is more likely to miss real delays.

Limitations and future work

Limitations and future work.

The main limitation is that the dataset is arrival-level but is not aware of the trip history. The model sees the scheduled hour, line, train type, station, and weather, but it does not see the train's earlier stops or whether it was already delayed upstream. This is likely why performance remains moderate even for the best model. A future version should reconstruct train trajectories and add upstream delay features.

A second limitation is the seasonal sampling design. The project uses four 30-day windows instead of a full year. This gives seasonal coverage, but it may miss unusual disruptions, holiday effects, construction periods, and extreme weather days outside those windows. A full-year sample would make the seasonal and weather findings more stable.

The weather merge is hourly, so it cannot capture short bursts of precipitation, platform-level conditions, or localized disruptions. Adding more granular weather, event, maintenance, and service-alert data could improve both explanation and prediction.

There are also modeling limitations. The final prediction target treats every positive delay as delayed, even if the delay is only one minute. A future extension could compare multiple thresholds, such as delays greater than 3, 5, or 10 minutes. This would better separate operationally meaningful delays from minor timetable noise.

Finally, the analysis is geographically focused on Lausanne. That makes the project coherent, but the conclusions should not automatically be generalized to all Swiss stations. A broader study could compare Lausanne with other stations and evaluate whether the same features matter across different rail network contexts.
